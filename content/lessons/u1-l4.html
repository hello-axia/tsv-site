You already know to be skeptical of an argument you don’t like. When someone says something that conflicts with what you believe, your brain automatically questions their stance. The defenses come up on their own.

None of those defenses arise when you agree.

When an argument lines up with what you already believe in, your brain becomes a generous reader. The weak parts get smoothed over, missing evidence doesn’t seem missed, and unspoken assumptions sound like common sense. You walk away more convinced of something you already thought, without thinking whether or not the argument actually made sense.

This is the most invisible bias we have: not bias against the other side, but bias for our own.

We are already good at critically analyzing the other side. But what matters more is critically analyzing our own thoughts. If we accept weak arguments because they feel right, we’ll end up with a worldview built on our biases.

Today, we’re going to do something most people never do, even as they get older: identify our biases.

The Argument

Read

The argument, as written

Teenagers, especially those in high school, should not have their social media access restricted by the government. Social media is how young people stay connected and join communities to find their passions. Taking it away will cut them off from each other in a way no previous generation could understand. The main reason supporters argue to take away social media access is the rising mental health crisis among teens. But there are other reasons for the rising crisis: school pressures, family stress, a slowing economy. Blaming social media is convenient, but it ignores the harder questions that teenagers face. Some teenagers are months away from being adults who will enter the real world and be able to participate politically. Treating them as incapable of managing their own digital lives on social media while they are so close to adulthood is condescending. Further, the laws being proposed are written by politicians who don’t use or understand the platforms they are suggesting to ban. If we trust them with their vote in just a couple months, we can trust them with social media.

The Analysis

Decode

As you read that paragraph, you probably agreed with at least some of the points that it makes. Maybe you agreed the whole way through. While the argument does have some good points, there are some points that need to be addressed.

Let’s separate the defensible claims from the biased claims that seem like arguments.

Defensible
Social media is how young people stay connected and find community. True. Anyone arguing for restriction has to address this real cost.
The mental health crisis has multiple causes — school, family, economy. True. Pinning everything on one cause is bad analysis.

Now the harder ones. Each of these sounds like an argument. Before you tap, ask yourself: would this hold up if the other side had said it?

If the same paragraph had argued in the opposite direction, would you have read it the same way? If instead the paragraph claimed that the teenager’s social media access should be restricted, would you have demanded evidence or noticed when an emotional appeal was standing in for an argument? If you would have been tougher on the other side, that is your bias. But it is not bad to have a bias! What we need to learn is to apply our analysis to situations where we intuitively agree.

The Habit

What to do with this

We are not going to stop having biases. Everyone does. The goal instead is to become suspicious of what we already agree with.
