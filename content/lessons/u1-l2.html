If you take a look at any headline in the news, you’ll likely see Americans fighting about something. Immigration. Guns. Taxes. Abortion. A new Supreme Court ruling. At a glance, it may seem like all of them are separate, as if the country is fighting about anything and everything under the sun.

The good news: there is a common thread connecting them all.

Underneath almost every political fight in the country, whether it be in the news or at the family gathering, you’ll find the same handful of disagreements. They may come up in different topics but the underlying question is usually one of six. Once you can recognize the questions, the political noise becomes far less complicated.

These are the Six Tensions of American Politics.

These are not problems to solve but rather trade-offs to consider. Each tension highlights where two values pull against each other, and the country must decide which one should have more weight at any given moment. Even highly educated people can fall in different places on every one of them.

The Six Tensions
1 Liberty vs. Equality. Should the system protect people’s freedom to act, or work to make outcomes fairer? Taxes, affirmative action, school funding, healthcare — this tension underlies all of them.
2 Liberty vs. Order. How much freedom do we give up for safety and stability? Surveillance laws, gun laws, drug policy, public health mandates, policing disputes — these are all variations of this tension.
3 Majority Rule vs. Minority Rights. Are there certain rights that are secured to the minority, even if the majority have decided in a fair vote? The Electoral College, religious exemptions, free speech protection on unpopular views, protection of immigrant rights — these tensions live here.
4 Individual vs. Community. Is the basic unit of politics the person, or the group they belong to? Mask mandates, zoning laws, gun rights, parental rights in schools — all of these ask whether your choice is yours, or do you also have responsibility for the people around you.
5 Centralized vs. Decentralized Power. Some policies sit at the national level, while others sit closer to home. Who decides? Federal government, state government, the school board? An example: abortion after Dobbs. The question is not “what is the policy” but “who gets to set the policy?”
6 Tradition vs. Progress. When do we keep what works, and when do we need to change it? Marriage laws, the Constitution, school curricula, the structure of the Senate — every reform debate has this tension nudged in it.

Most political arguments you’ll hear in your life are probably not about the surface topic. They are about one of these tensions, and the split in the country comes from which value they prioritize.

Two people arguing about gun laws may actually be fighting about Liberty vs. Order or Individual vs. Community. People fighting about immigration laws might actually be fighting about Majority Rule vs. Minority Rights or Liberty vs. Order.

Once you can identify the tensions, the common rage-baiting tactics each side uses will not tempt you.

Throughout the year, we will work on being able to identify all six. But today, we will start with one.
