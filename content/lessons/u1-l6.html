The question

Should public schools have the authority to search a student's phone when they suspect a school rule has been broken?

This question sits on the tension of Liberty vs. Order. Student privacy on one side; school authority and safety on the other. Both sides have constitutional weight, real cases behind them, and real costs.

The legal foundation

In New Jersey v. T.L.O. (1985), the Supreme Court ruled that public school officials may search a student's belongings without a warrant if they have "reasonable suspicion" that a school rule or law has been broken. This is a lower standard than the "probable cause" that applies to adults. The Court reasoned that schools have a special responsibility to maintain order and protect students, and that responsibility justifies the lower bar.

The decision rests on a doctrine called in loco parentis — "in place of the parent." When a student is at school, the school takes on some of the responsibilities a parent would have. A parent does not need a warrant to look inside their child's backpack. Under this doctrine, neither does a school.

What "reasonable suspicion" looks like in practice
Reasonable
A teacher sees a vape sticking out of a student's backpack. That justifies a search.
Not reasonable
A teacher searches a student because they "seem off" or "have an attitude."
Borderline
Another student anonymously reports that a classmate has drugs in their bag. Courts have split on whether anonymous tips clear the bar.

The phone problem

The Supreme Court has not ruled directly on whether the T.L.O. standard applies to phone searches. The most relevant case for adults is Riley v. California (2014), where the Court ruled unanimously that even after a lawful arrest, police need a warrant to search a phone. Chief Justice Roberts wrote that phones are categorically different from other personal items — searching one is a far greater intrusion than searching a wallet or a purse.

Riley applies to adults and the police; it does not directly apply to schools and students. But it raises the question:

If a phone is too personal for police to search without a warrant, can a school search a student's phone on mere "reasonable suspicion"?

What schools actually do

Administrators who have searched student phones most often cite reasons like these:

  • Investigating threats to other students — threats of violence, threats made over social media, threats surfaced through anonymous reports.
  • Following up on cyberbullying complaints — the harassment itself often happened in messages that only exist on the phone.
  • Threat assessment — since the 2018 Parkland shooting, most U.S. districts have adopted formal threat assessment protocols. When a student makes a threat, the protocol typically requires reviewing their communications, which is often only possible with phone access.
  • Investigating coordinated fights or assaults — phones are now where fights are planned, recorded, and circulated.
  • Suspicion of drug-related activity — messages may confirm or refute the suspicion.
  • Suspicion of cheating — especially photographed tests or answer-sharing in group chats.

The pattern: most school discipline questions today cannot be investigated without access to digital communications.

What those searches typically turn up:

  • Vapes, drugs, or evidence of drug use.
  • Messages that confirm or refute the original suspicion.
  • Evidence of cyberbullying — toward the searched student, or by them toward others.
  • In rare but high-stakes cases: evidence of weapons, planned violence, or self-harm.
  • Sometimes nothing related to the original suspicion.
  • Sometimes things unrelated but separately concerning — intimate photos, mental health crisis content, or evidence of a crime the student was the victim of.

Two rulings, two directions

G.C. v. Owensboro Public Schools (6th Circuit, 2013) — a ruling against the school. A Kentucky high schooler was caught texting in class. An administrator confiscated the phone and read through several messages, looking for evidence of drug use because the student had a prior history. The Sixth Circuit ruled the search unconstitutional: texting in class is a rule violation, but it did not give the school reasonable suspicion to read through the student's messages. The court drew a line between confiscating a phone (allowed) and searching its contents (which requires its own justification).

Jackson v. McCurry (11th Circuit, 2019) — a ruling for the school. A senior was accused of bullying a classmate by text. Two students testified that she had been sending mean messages. The assistant principal searched her phone over her objection. The search came up empty, and the phone was returned. The student sued. The 11th Circuit ruled the search did not violate clearly established law: because the alleged conduct (bullying) was itself a rule violation, and because specific testimony pointed to the phone as the medium, the administrators had reasonable grounds.

The bigger picture

Over the last five years, state legislatures have moved in both directions. The proposals fall into a few camps:

  • Tighten restrictions — require parental notification before a search, raise the standard from "reasonable suspicion" to "probable cause," or limit a search to the specific time period and subject under investigation.
  • Expand authority for safety — strengthen schools' power to investigate threats; several states now shield schools from liability for threat-related searches conducted in good faith.
  • Require formal procedures — document the basis for a search, its scope, and what was found.

The Supreme Court has not stepped in, so the landscape is unsettled. Both sides of this deliberation have precedent to stand on.
