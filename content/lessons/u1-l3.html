“The minimum wage should be raised to $20 an hour.”

The claim that the minimum wage needs to be raised has persisted for generations. But the two sides who argue this claim seem to be addressing something different. On one side, the main concern is whether or not it will work — whether prices would spike, whether entry-level jobs would disappear, and whether it will actually help out the workers. On the other, the focal point seems to be what workers deserve — whether working full-time at a minimum wage job should be enough to live on.

These two are not the same argument. In fact, they aren’t even the same kind of argument. Until we can differentiate the two kinds, we cannot come to a conclusion on this debate.

The Distinction

Two Kinds of Questions

This is the most useful distinction in political reasoning.

Empirical questions are about what is. They have answers determined by evidence. An example is: Does raising the minimum wage cause unemployment? This is a question about the world that can be examined. Economists can study it and in principle have a correct answer.

Normative questions are about what should be. They have answers determined by values. A question like Should a full-time job pay enough to live on? does not have any evidence that can support it. Two people looking at these same facts can settle on two different conclusions.

Two kinds of questions
Empirical About what is. Settled (in principle) by evidence.
Normative About what should be. Argued with reasons and values.
The Application

Untangling the Wage Debate

Applying this to the original claim — “The minimum wage should be raised to $20 an hour” — we can see the tangle of two arguments stacked on top of each other.

The Empirical Thread

Would raising it actually help workers? Supporters of raising the minimum wage say a modest wage increase lifts incomes and well-being of workers without causing much job loss. Opponents find that businesses cut hours, raise prices, and make the general cost of living higher. Economists have swung both ways. The conclusion seems to be unclear.

The Normative Thread

Even if we knew what would happen, what do we owe workers? Is the wage floor a basic protection or government overreach? Supporters say a person who spends 40 hours a week working is owed at least enough to survive.

Opponents see wages as a private agreement between an employer and a worker — if the government sets the price, it is an overreach of power. There is no evidence that can support or deny either of these claims.

But this does not mean normative claims are unarguable. Value claims have answers that can be defended and challenged. You can press the supporter’s view by asking why dignity gets enforced at the wage floor and not elsewhere — why not universal housing, healthcare, or transportation? The line at wages starts to look arbitrary. You can question the opponent’s view by asking whether a worker and a billion-dollar employer really meet as equals when discussing wages. Is it really a “private agreement,” or is it the more powerful party setting terms the less powerful one has no real choice but to accept?

Once the threads are separated, both sides can stop talking past each other. The empirical disagreement gets argued with evidence. The normative disagreement gets argued with reasons.

Once you can see this distinction, you start seeing it everywhere.
