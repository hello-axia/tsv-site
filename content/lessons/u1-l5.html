In lesson 1.4, you learned to spot your own bias when you agree with something. Today, we're going to do something in the opposite direction: how to think clearly about something you disagree with.

When we encounter a political position we reject, our brains tend to do the same thing: build the weakest possible version of the other side's argument and knock it down. This is called strawmanning. We often win the argument in our heads when we scroll past a video that we disagree with. But the reality is: we won against a version of the argument no one actually holds.

The Harder Move

Steelmanning

The opposite move is called steelmanning. A steelman is the strongest possible version of an argument. Steelmanning is something we naturally avoid because it forces us to admit that the other person might be right — and we maybe, just maybe, might be wrong.

You don't know what you believe until you can accurately say what you disagree with.

Why steelmanning matters

If you can't make the other person's case, you probably don't understand the issue. Your own position can only be as strong as your grasp of what you're rejecting.

You can't actually persuade without steelmanning. You can never convince someone if you don't even know what you are arguing against.

Democracy depends on it. When citizens only know the strawman, we no longer debate. We throw insults because we think the other party is just down-right stupid.
